A short guide

The Art of Storytelling

Long before writing, people sat around fires and told each other stories. We still do. Here is why they work, and how to tell one well.

Why stories matter

Facts tell us what happened. Stories tell us what it meant. A good story lets a listener live through someone else's experience for a few minutes, and people tend to remember what they have felt far longer than what they have been told.

Stories pass on values, explain the world, build trust between strangers, and make a case without sounding like an argument. Teachers, leaders, marketers and parents all lean on them for the same reason.

"There is no greater agony than bearing an untold story inside you." Maya Angelou

The shape of a story

Most stories, from fairy tales to films, follow a familiar arc:

  1. The setupIntroduce a character and their ordinary world.
  2. The disruptionSomething changes. A want, a problem, a door opens.
  3. The struggleObstacles rise. Each attempt costs something.
  4. The turning pointA choice or discovery that changes everything.
  5. The resolutionA new normal, and a character who is different for the journey.

Tips for telling it well

Start late

Open as close to the action as you can. Skip the warm-up.

Show, don't tell

"Her hands shook" beats "she was nervous."

Make them care

Give your character a clear want and something at stake.

Use specifics

One vivid detail is worth a paragraph of general description.

Build tension

Hold back answers. Curiosity keeps people listening.

Know your ending

Decide where you're going before you start, then cut what doesn't lead there.

Try it yourself

Think of a moment when something didn't go as planned. Tell it in under a minute: who you were, what you wanted, what went wrong, and what you learned. That's a story.

Every story starts with someone willing to tell it.
